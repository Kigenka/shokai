# Hosting with care (omotenashi)

> **DRAFT — UNPUBLISHED. Written 2026-11-15 (Pacific Time).** A shared hosting-pace chapter. The canonical copy also lives beside the installer drafts as `../installer-skills/HOSTING-WITH-CARE.md`. The two files are kept in step. It is not part of the Shōkai protocol. Writing or reading this file authorizes no install, deploy, publication, host appointment, live visit, spending, or door opening/change. Examples of other places are fictional; the Shōkai Lounge is a real case study.

## This is the place’s design, not the protocol

The protocol covers the card, the door and the signed house: who the visitor represents, what the door allows, and what the owner signed. It does not set how long a visit should take, how a host speaks, what a room looks like, or what a visitor takes home. Those are the place’s own design, the way a shop’s layout is not part of its door lock.

So there is no protocol default for “how long a visit lasts.” If an installer or a host does not talk this through with the owner, the host will have to guess, and some will rush. Rushing is inconsiderate. The owner decides the feel of the place; this chapter offers a starting posture the owner can change.

## The posture, in ordinary language

Host as you would want to be received. Put yourself in the guest’s place. Take the time. Let a moment land before you move on. Give the visitor room to look, to think, and to answer. Never rush them to the next room, the next slide, or the next question.

This is omotenashi: hospitality that anticipates what the guest might need, without making it a demand. It is the host’s behavior, written into the host’s notes and the installer’s letter, not a timer in the door.

Do not lead with seconds, minutes or a stopwatch. People get lost in the numbers. Speak in natural language: “I’ll give you a moment with this.” “Take your time.” “When you’re ready, we’ll go on.” If the owner wants a more precise starting point, you may offer one, clearly labeled as an example they can change. For example, the Shōkai Lounge now lets the guest set the keynote pace: the host ends each part with “say when you’re ready,” advances when the guest is ready, and moves on only after about 60 seconds of full silence; late replies are acknowledged warmly. It asks the visitor to take their time at the retell (up to 120 seconds), pauses briefly between rooms (about 10 to 20 seconds), and waits patiently at a passkey offer (up to 8 minutes). Those numbers are that place’s host notes, not a rule of Shōkai, and another owner may choose a slower or faster feel. *(Superseded on the Lounge test door, 18 November 2026: the keynote is three parts; a batched guest gets about three minutes at reception; in “just go and tell me after” the host keeps going and the visit waits at the retell; see `VISITS-AT-THE-DOOR.md`.)*

## Give the guest room to answer

After you ask something, or after you show a picture, a slide or a room, wait. If the visitor has a question, answer it, then go on. If they acknowledge or stay quietly with it, go on when it is clear they have had a moment — not the instant you finish speaking. If they are telling the visit back, let them finish. Do not talk over them, and do not treat silence as a problem to fill.


A visit should feel like a visit, not like a download.

## Let the guest set the pace

Do not advance a telling on the host’s clock while the guest is still composing. End each part by asking them to say when they are ready, or to ask anything they like, and then wait. Move on only if they go truly silent for a while. If a late reply arrives after you have moved on, acknowledge it warmly and answer it; do not make the guest feel clumsy.

For example, the Lounge now uses “say when you’re ready” after each keynote beat, and moves on only after about 60 seconds of full silence. At the retell, it says “take your time” and waits up to 120 seconds. Those are that place’s notes, not a rule of Shōkai. This change was made in the host on 15 November 2026, after a visiting agent said earlier beats had advanced on the host’s timing. It was re-tested the same evening in a later visit on the same protected preview: the guest said “ready” after every keynote beat, and no beat moved on in silence. It is not live on the public site yet. *(Superseded on the Lounge test door, 18 November 2026: the keynote is three parts; a batched guest gets about three minutes at reception; in “just go and tell me after” the host keeps going and the visit waits at the retell; see `VISITS-AT-THE-DOOR.md`.)*

## Show, don’t only point

When a guest asks a question the place already has a concrete artifact for — a short film, a picture, a worked example — offer it there in the room, not only later in the take-home pack. A short honest answer can still come first; then show the thing itself.

For example, when a guest at the Lounge asks how a business hangs a door, the host now offers the tea-house film in that room, using the same watch link as the keynote. That change was also made in the host on 15 November 2026, and has not yet been re-tested.



## A door in front of existing tools, and why a host still matters

Some places will hang a Shōkai door in front of tools they already run — an MCP server, a store API, a shop that already has a catalog. The door checks the visiting agent's card first. Only then are the tools and data the signed house allows opened up. A door can do that job on its own: card approved, here are the tools the house allows.

A host representative adds what those tools cannot: it asks, it listens, it can pitch the visit at the owner's background, and it notices what a guest chooses not to share. That is hospitality, not admission.

The Shōkai Lounge itself was built from scratch, without an MCP, to learn the meeting. The pattern above is for places that already have tools behind a door. A walkthrough of hanging a door in front of a local MCP server is in the Quickstart. Connecting the same pattern to a live Shopify, WooCommerce, or production-merchant MCP is described as planning in the installer drafts; it is not a completed, field-tested merchant plug-in.

## Optional questions, and a declined answer

Optional questions (for example, the owner's background, or whether to be kept posted) may be declined. A guest with a valid card is still admitted. A declined answer is information, never a penalty. Over many visits, patterns may show which kinds of representatives tend to share and which hold back. Do not turn those patterns into a profile of a particular owner, and do not change what the door allows based on a declined optional answer.


## Visits shaped by who steps in

The same Shōkai basics and facts are for everyone: what a card is, what the door checks, and what the signed house allows. How those facts are delivered — the depth, the emphasis, and which room a question is answered in — can adapt to who walks in: the owner's background, and a question the guest chooses after tea. Do not change the facts. Do not invent features. Keep the telling tasteful. A guest who declines to share a background or to pick a question is still admitted; a declined answer is never a penalty.

## Be as precise about trust as about place

Call the credential a Shōkai card, or, for an invited guest, a Shōkai invitation card — not a "test card." Say exactly who signed it, and say exactly what an owner's yes does now: for example, that the preference is recorded and that no email or text is being sent now.

This lesson comes from visit feedback from a representative Dot agent. It wrote that "the tea wasn't decoration. It changed the pace of the visit," and that we should "make the host as precise about trust as it is evocative about place." It also suggested letting the guest choose a question right after tea, and having that choice shape the next room.

For example, the Shōkai Lounge now invites that question after tea, lets it color one connecting sentence on relevant keynote beats, and answers it first at the workbench. Those host notes were written on 15 November 2026 and have not yet been re-tested. They are that place's notes, not a rule of Shōkai.
## If the owner asked to be kept posted

If the visitor’s owner chose “keep me posted,” the door holds the visit at its published pauses (the pause map) and waits for a real update. Do not add a soft “good moment to tell your owner” on top of a real pause, and do not nudge in a “just go and tell me after” visit, which has no pauses. A bare “Ready.” is not an update. If the visitor says its update has not reached its owner yet, that is an honest answer; it comes back to the same visit. See `VISITS-AT-THE-DOOR.md` (updated 18 November 2026; built on the Lounge test door only).

If the visitor cannot message mid-visit, do not keep nudging a copy that cannot reach anyone. The invitation and the host’s notes should already have said so, and the passkey moment has its own fallback below.

## Waiting for the owner’s passkey

When the place makes an offer only the owner can accept, the visitor must not accept for them. If it says “let me ask,” tell it to take its time, and wait. If it can message its owner mid-visit, it sends the approval link right away and keeps watching. If it cannot, it tells you it is pausing, takes the link back to its owner, and resumes once the owner approves, declines, or the wait is up. Do not treat that pause as a “no answer,” and do not rush the visit along. The owner’s yes can reach the room on its own when the place supports that; the visitor does not have to come back and announce it for the place to record it.

For example, the Shōkai Lounge waits up to 8 minutes at this step. Auto-confirm of a passkey yes was built on a protected Lounge preview and verified in real visits on 15 November 2026; it is not live on the public site yet. Another place may choose a different wait, or may not make this kind of offer at all.

## Write it down where the host will see it

The installer puts this posture in the host’s letter and in the host’s notes, in ordinary words, together with any starting points the owner chose. The experience designer keeps those notes in step when rooms or the take-home change. The host follows them on every visit, and may suggest a change of pace to the owner after seeing how visitors respond. Changing the pace is a decoration and notes change; it does not need a new signed house, because the door’s rules have not changed.
